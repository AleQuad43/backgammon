2Why √N

Think of a match to N as a sum of roughly N independent games, each giving the stronger player a small edge δ in expected points per game with standard deviation σ. By the central limit theorem:

The match "z-score" grows like √N. A probit and a logistic are nearly the same curve (σ(1.7x) ≈ Φ(x)), so putting √N inside the logistic is the same statement. Doubling the length does not double the edge; quadrupling it does.

The constant

Only the 10 is a choice. With a fixed step, ratings never fully settle: they jitter around the truth with a spread that grows like √K. Larger K tracks a player who improves faster; smaller K gives steadier numbers. For about 10 friends playing a few matches a week, 10√N is a reasonable middle.

4What the margin costs

We score a win by its margin instead of 1:

With a constant step, the ratings settle where the expected step is zero:

With binary S, E[S] = p, the true win probability, so E is calibrated. With margins, E converges to the expected share, which is pulled toward ½:

What we lose: E as a literal win probability. A player who wins 70% of matches but usually narrowly has E[S] ≈ 0.62, so the gap reads like a 62% favourite.
What we keep: convergence, and the ranking order, as long as stronger players also win by bigger margins, which in backgammon they do.
Small gain: S is less extreme, so ratings are a little less noisy.

Formally, using a fractional S turns the Bernoulli likelihood into a quasi-likelihood, ES(1−E)1−S: a "soft label", like label smoothing. The machinery below still works, but the posterior is a pseudo-posterior.

Concession

A conceded match is scored as if the winner reached N and the conceder kept their score. Conceding at 3–4 in a match to 5 gives Swin = 0.75 + 0.25·(2/5) = 0.85. That only makes sense with margins, which is why the two decisions go together.

6Newcomers

A newcomer starts with a wide prior (σ₀ large). From the update in section 5, the step each side takes is proportional to their own variance:

  • The newcomer moves a lot: the result is informative about them.
  • The established player moves less than usual, because gj < 1: a result against an unknown says little about them.

So the instinct "if the newcomer speeds up, the others must too" points the wrong way. It also explains what we give up: the updates are no longer equal and opposite, so the pool's mean isn't conserved. That's expected; the mean has no meaning by itself.

Simple Elo version: the newcomer uses 2K for their first 5 matches; their opponent uses normal K. That's a two-level stand-in for σ.
Full version: keep (μ, σ) per player and use section 5 directly. It's about 15 lines of code, and the app could show a rating as "1540 ± 80", which a Bayesian will appreciate.
